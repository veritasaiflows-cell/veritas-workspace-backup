# Workflow 75 - AI Drop-Service OS Training Plan

## Purpose

This is Randall's practical training track for operating the AI Drop-Service OS. The goal is not to make Randall a developer. The goal is to make Randall capable of owning the offer, steering the agents, spotting bad claims, handling buyer discovery, and approving the right next actions.

## Randall's Role

Randall is the owner/operator, not a passive observer.

Core responsibilities:

- choose the target niche or approve Veritas's recommendation
- approve the offer, price test, scope, and risk boundaries
- talk to prospects when external action is approved
- provide business judgment on pain, urgency, buyer language, and trust
- approve or reject customer-facing claims
- decide when to move from internal demo to private pilot
- protect the system from hype, fake certainty, and over-promising

Things Randall does not need to do first:

- write code
- configure every tool personally
- manually inspect every helper output
- become an expert in every no-code platform before the first internal demo

## Operating Model Randall Needs To Understand

OpenClaw works as a command center:

- Veritas main owns final truth and integration.
- Helper agents produce bounded drafts, research, templates, QA, or proof.
- Helper output is not trusted until Veritas verifies it.
- Team memory tracks accepted state, open tasks, and next safe actions.
- Long-work packets preserve original request, current state, proof, and recovery after compaction or failure.
- Cron/watchdogs should refresh review surfaces first, not silently mutate the business.

The core question before any automation is: what can safely run without Randall, and what still needs owner approval?

## Training Track

### Module 1: AI Drop-Service Basics

Learn:

- product dropshipping versus AI drop servicing
- why OpenClaw is better suited to services than physical ecommerce
- what a fixed-scope outcome offer is
- why generic "AI agency" positioning is weak
- how proof-backed delivery becomes the moat

Practice:

- explain the AI Workflow Clarity Sprint in one sentence
- name three business pains it can solve
- name three things it must not promise

### Module 2: Buyer And Offer Design

Learn:

- target buyer, painful workflow, measurable outcome, and scope boundary
- difference between a feature, deliverable, and business result
- value-based pricing logic without fake ROI claims
- why first offers should be narrow

Practice:

- choose one target business type
- write a plain-English problem statement
- write the offer in this shape: "We help [buyer] fix [workflow pain] by delivering [specific artifact/outcome] in [timeframe]."

### Module 3: Discovery And Sales Conversations

Learn:

- how to ask workflow-diagnosis questions
- how to listen for urgency, budget, authority, and trust
- how to avoid selling tools instead of outcomes
- what information OpenClaw needs to build a useful packet

Practice questions:

- Where do new leads or requests enter today?
- What gets dropped, delayed, or repeated manually?
- What happens when someone is unavailable?
- Which follow-ups are inconsistent?
- What would be worth fixing first if we only had two weeks?

### Module 4: OpenClaw Multi-Agent Operation

Learn:

- how to ask Veritas for a helper lane
- what Research Scout, Offer Architect, Workflow Engineer, QA Red-Team, and Continuity Clerk do
- why agents need stop lines
- why persistent agents should come after logical role proof
- how to read a closeout: accepted, rejected, unverified, blocked

Practice commands in plain language:

- "Spawn a Research Scout to find public examples of workflow audit offers."
- "Have QA Red-Team challenge this offer for over-promising."
- "Create a workflow-engineer packet for a fictional missed-lead demo."
- "Update the team board with accepted facts only."

### Module 5: Delivery Workflow

Learn the service delivery sequence:

1. Intake
2. Workflow map
3. Pain and bottleneck diagnosis
4. Automation opportunity matrix
5. Risk and dependency list
6. Implementation backlog
7. Optional small prototype
8. QA/red-team pass
9. Closeout report

Randall's checkpoint:

- Is the problem real?
- Is the recommendation understandable?
- Would a business owner trust this?
- Are we promising only what we can prove?

### Module 6: Claims, Privacy, And Quality

Learn:

- advertising claims must be truthful and evidence-backed
- no fake testimonials, fake case studies, or guaranteed revenue
- no private customer data without explicit scope
- no client credentials or system access without separate approval
- AI output needs review before customer-facing use

Practice:

- rewrite hype into evidence-backed language
- identify privacy-sensitive fields in a fake client brief
- mark which claims need proof

### Module 7: Trends To Watch

Watch these trends:

- vertical AI agents for specific business workflows
- service-as-software: packaged workflows sold like services but delivered with software/AI
- lead response automation for local service businesses
- AI receptionists, voice agents, and appointment routing
- CRM cleanup, enrichment, and follow-up automation
- AI search and SEO shifts affecting local businesses
- no-code/low-code automation through Zapier, Make, n8n, Airtable, Notion, HubSpot, GoHighLevel, and similar tools
- privacy and compliance expectations around customer data
- buyer fatigue around vague AI promises

What matters most:

- specific outcome beats generic AI
- vertical focus beats "we automate anything"
- proof beats hype
- fast diagnosis plus clear implementation path beats tool demos

## Suggested 14-Day Training Sprint

Days 1-2:

- Learn the model and read this plan.
- Choose one likely first niche.
- Draft the one-sentence offer.

Days 3-4:

- Run a fictional discovery interview.
- Build the first missed-lead demo brief.

Days 5-7:

- Create workflow map, opportunity matrix, and closeout report v0.
- QA pass for claims, privacy, and feasibility.

Days 8-10:

- Learn the agent roles.
- Practice asking Veritas for Research Scout, QA Red-Team, and Workflow Engineer outputs.

Days 11-12:

- Review trends and competitor examples.
- Refine the offer and scope.

Days 13-14:

- Decide whether to create the first persistent isolated agents.
- Decide whether the internal demo is strong enough to prepare private pilot materials.

## Readiness Checklist

Randall is ready for private-pilot prep when he can:

- explain the offer in one sentence
- identify the buyer and pain
- run a basic discovery conversation
- distinguish internal demo from customer-ready deliverable
- identify claims that need evidence
- explain what each agent does
- understand what actions are owner-gated
- reject hype even when it sounds profitable
