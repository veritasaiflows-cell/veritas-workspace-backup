So the label is **not** stale garbage. It is a precise **owner-tracked deployment-candidate set** that was misnamed `tier` and therefore got wired into routing. Reconciling it to routed tiers would strip decision-grade freshness from seven names with live stops — the actively harmful option, and the one my first message pointed toward.

Also found: the same label is duplicated across **four** columns — `tier`, `sql_tier`, `tier_decision_scope`, `decision_grade_eligible` (all 15/17/268, `sql_tier` disagrees with the router on 65 tickers). Four chances to desync.

Standing recommendation for Decision 1: keep the set, kill the word "tier", derive it from the portfolio surface with a validator asserting every written-band+stop ticker is in it, collapse the four columns to one, and give the 42 routed-A/B-but-unlabeled names a separate **non-blocking** promotion-review freshness lane.

### Durable lessons
- **The real design defect was one flag serving two questions:** *"can staleness here mislead capital?"* (owner-tracked, blocking) vs *"is this fresh enough to evaluate for promotion?"* (merit, non-blocking). Collapsing those into one field is what made the label look simultaneously stale and load-bearing.
- **Check what a label actually predicts before calling it stale.** I nearly recommended retiring a field that was 100% accurate for its real purpose. The test that saved it was cross-referencing against written stops, not reading the field's name.
- **A merit ranker doesn't know what you own.** The router scores 300 names on momentum/fundamentals; it has no idea which 20 carry capital commitment. Never let an evidence ranker silently redefine an ownership set.
- **Verify ticker regex hits against the source line.** `MA`, `IR`, `EPS`, `UP`, `POST`, `DATE` all parse as tickers. One false positive would have put a fake gap in a recommendation.
